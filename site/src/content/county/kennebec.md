Kennebec County is the capital county, and it behaves like two places at once. Augusta and Waterville sit on the Kennebec River with older housing, a lot of it rented or converted into apartments. Beyond them the county opens into lakes, farmland and woodland, with camps and year-round homes on wooded lots. Both of our confirmed jobs here were in the cities, but the tick numbers come from the countryside.

## River cities: rats, mice and shared buildings

In Augusta and Waterville the calls cluster around older and subdivided buildings. Rats are the clearest example. The Augusta job we have on record involved rats digging beneath a trailer and getting in from below. Nothing inside the trailer would have fixed that. What worked was outside: secured bait stations placed where the rats were travelling, and the burrow entrances filled so the colony had nowhere to retreat to. The same logic applies to sheds, decks and any outbuilding sitting on soil — the [rats in the basement](/services/rodent-control/rats-in-the-basement/) page goes into how rats get in from the ground up.

Bed bugs are the other city problem. In Waterville a property-management company reported one unit. The inspection found that the infestation was heavy and had already reached the connecting apartments, so all of the affected units were treated together until it was gone. In a converted house or a small apartment block, the wall between two units is not a barrier to a bed bug; it is a corridor. The [bed bug control](/services/bed-bug-control/) and [commercial pest control](/services/commercial-pest-control/) pages cover how that kind of job is scoped for landlords.

The Census Bureau's 2020–2024 survey counts just over a quarter of the county's occupied homes as rented. Inside the two cities the Bureau's own figures put the rented share far higher — close to half in Augusta and over half in Waterville — and that is where most of the shared-wall work happens.

## Lake country: camps and the long empty season

The same survey counts 6,070 homes in Kennebec held for seasonal, recreational or occasional use. Whether it is a lakeside camp or a second home, an unheated building that nobody visits between autumn and spring is a reliable place for mice to overwinter, and the owner usually finds out on the first visit of the year, when the nesting and droppings are already there. Closing a camp properly — exterior stations set, gaps at the sill and plumbing sealed, soft goods stored in sealed bins — costs less than cleaning one out. See [rodent exclusion](/services/rodent-exclusion/) and, where the insulation has been soiled, [attic clean-outs and sanitation](/services/attic-cleanout-and-sanitation/).

## Ticks: Central Maine is catching up

Maine CDC's 2024 tickborne illness report describes Central Maine as quickly emerging as a third area of increased Lyme incidence, after the Midcoast and Downeast. Kennebec's own numbers show it: 295 cases among residents in 2024 at a rate of 229.6 per 100,000, against 125 cases and a rate of 102.2 in 2020. The statewide rate for 2024 was 230.0, so the county now sits squarely at the state average. The report also lists 112 anaplasmosis and 41 babesiosis cases here, plus one of the state's Powassan virus cases and two cases of spotted fever rickettsiosis.

The UMaine Cooperative Extension Tick Lab's 2025 report adds the tick-side view. It tested 275 deer ticks sent in from Kennebec County, and 115 of them — 41.8% — carried the Lyme disease bacterium; 13.8% carried Babesia microti. These are ticks people found and posted to the lab, not a field survey, so the numbers show what is out there rather than measuring it precisely.

For a property backing onto woods or field edges, [tick control](/services/tick-control/) treats the edge habitat on a schedule through the warm months, and the [ticks in the yard](/services/tick-control/ticks-in-the-yard/) page covers the habitat work an owner can do alongside it.
