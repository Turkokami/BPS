Oxford County is big, wooded and lightly populated, and a large share of its buildings are empty for much of the year. That single fact shapes more pest work here than anything else. The U.S. Census Bureau's 2020–2024 American Community Survey counts 59,255 residents and 36,855 housing units — and 10,568 of those units are held for seasonal, recreational or occasional use. More than one home in every four is a camp, cottage or second home.

## Why the empty building is the job

A camp shut up in October offers a mouse almost everything it needs for winter: shelter from the wind, no people, no cat, mattresses and cushions to shred for nesting, and often a pantry shelf with something left on it. Nobody walks through the building for months, so nothing disturbs the nest and nothing reports the problem until the owners come back in spring to droppings, chewed wiring and soiled insulation.

Camp construction often makes entry easy. A camp on posts or piers rather than a full foundation leaves the underside of the floor open to the ground. Where the plumbing is drained for winter, the holes around pipes passing through the floor are still there. Screened porches, add-on rooms and decades of owner repairs leave seams that nobody has looked at closely. None of that is unusual, and all of it is fixable, but it has to be found before it can be sealed.

The fix is cheaper before closing than after opening. A closing-up visit looks for the gaps at the sill, around plumbing and electrical entries, under the deck and at the roofline; sets secured stations outside; and leaves the owner a short list of what to store in sealed bins. An opening-up visit in spring checks whether anything got through. Where it already has, [attic clean-outs and sanitation](/services/attic-cleanout-and-sanitation/) deals with the contaminated insulation, and [rodent exclusion](/services/rodent-exclusion/) closes the ways in.

Year-round homes here are different again, and the Census figures show how different the county's two halves are. With owner occupancy at 80.6% by the Census Bureau's count, most of the county's occupied houses are lived in by the people who own them, many on wooded lots with outbuildings, woodpiles and long boundaries against the trees. That brings carpenter ants into damp sills and porch framing, and wasps into eaves and sheds over the summer. For those houses, a [residential pest control](/services/residential-pest-control/) plan that comes back through the season usually makes more sense than a single call-out, because the same woodland edge keeps supplying the same pests.

## Ticks on wooded lots

Maine CDC's 2024 tickborne illness report counts 131 Lyme disease cases among Oxford County residents, a rate of 218.2 per 100,000. In 2020 the same series recorded 43 cases at a rate of 74.2, so the rate has nearly tripled in four years. The county was also one of three in Maine to record a spotted fever rickettsiosis case in 2024, and the report lists 52 anaplasmosis and 10 babesiosis cases among residents.

The UMaine Cooperative Extension Tick Lab tested 109 deer ticks submitted from the county in 2025; 44 of them, or 40.4%, carried the Lyme disease bacterium. The lab points out that its samples come from people who chose to send ticks in, so they show what is circulating rather than measuring it.

On a property where the lawn meets woodland, [tick control](/services/tick-control/) treats that edge on a schedule through the season, and the [ticks in the yard](/services/tick-control/ticks-in-the-yard/) page lists the habitat changes — leaf litter, brush, wood storage — that make the treatment hold.
