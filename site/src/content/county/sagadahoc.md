Sagadahoc County is small — 37,285 residents in 19,239 housing units, by the U.S. Census Bureau's 2020–2024 American Community Survey — but it sits on the Midcoast, and in Maine that means tick pressure well above the state average. It also has some of the oldest housing in our service area. Both of those facts shape the work here more than the county's size does.

## Ticks: the Midcoast pattern

Maine CDC's 2024 tickborne illness report is blunt about where the disease is concentrated. The Midcoast and Downeast have the highest Lyme incidence in the state, and the four Midcoast counties — Knox, Lincoln, Sagadahoc and Waldo — accounted for 29% of all reported cases in 2024. Sagadahoc is one of eight counties whose rate was above the state's for the second year in a row.

The county's own numbers are striking. Maine CDC counted 27 Lyme cases among residents in 2020, at 75.3 per 100,000; in 2024 it counted 139, at 369.9. The same report lists 76 anaplasmosis and 14 babesiosis cases in the county last year.

The UMaine Cooperative Extension Tick Lab's 2025 report shows what the ticks themselves are carrying. Of the 124 deer ticks it tested from Sagadahoc, 45 (36.3%) carried the Lyme disease bacterium and 3 (2.4%) carried Powassan virus. That Powassan share was the highest of any county in the report; the statewide figure was 0.8%. Three ticks is a small number, and the lab notes that its samples are sent in by the public rather than collected in a survey, so it is a signal rather than a measurement. It is still a signal worth acting on: the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention notes that Powassan virus can cause severe illness, including inflammation of the brain.

For a yard that meets woods, brush or overgrown field, [tick control](/services/tick-control/) treats the edge habitat on a seasonal schedule, and the [ticks in the yard](/services/tick-control/ticks-in-the-yard/) page explains which yard changes reduce the number of ticks waiting there. Daily checks after time outdoors matter more here than almost anywhere else we work.

## Old houses and new owners

More than a quarter of the county's housing — 27.1% — was built in 1939 or earlier, the highest share among the five counties where we have work on record, according to the Census Bureau's survey. Old timber-framed houses come with old problems: sills that have taken on water, gaps where additions meet the original structure, and foundations with decades of patched penetrations. Those are the conditions that let mice in and give carpenter ants damp wood to nest in.

The inspection on a house like that is slow on purpose. It walks the sill line and the bulkhead, looks hard at the joint between the original house and any ell or addition, checks where the water, power and oil lines come through the foundation, and follows the roofline for soffit gaps and loose vent screens. Inside, it looks for the evidence that says which pest and how long: droppings and their age, frass below a window frame, rub marks along a beam. The report then separates what we treat from what needs a carpenter.

The job on record here was exactly that kind of house. New owners in Bath found ants and mice soon after buying, and had already treated the place themselves with store-bought aerosols before they called. That is a very common first move, and an understandable one, but sprays tend to scatter ants and push mice into quieter parts of the building, which makes the source harder to find. An inspection that traces the trails back to their entry points comes first; [rodent exclusion](/services/rodent-exclusion/) and [ant control](/services/ant-control/) follow from what it finds.
