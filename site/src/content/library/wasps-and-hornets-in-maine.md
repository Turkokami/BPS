Maine's stinging-insect season is short and it escalates. A queen starts a nest in April or May, the colony is small and barely noticeable through June, and by August it is at maximum size and at its most defensive — which is exactly when people are outside.

## Telling them apart

| Insect | Nest | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paper wasp | Open, umbrella-shaped comb under eaves, rails, grills | Least aggressive; stings when the nest is disturbed |
| Yellowjacket | Enclosed, in the ground, in wall voids, in sheds | Most likely to sting in numbers; scavenges food and drink outdoors in late summer |
| Bald-faced hornet | Large grey enclosed paper nest, often in a tree or on a wall | Very defensive close to the nest |
| Mud dauber | Small mud tubes on walls and in sheds | Solitary, rarely stings; generally leave them |
| Carpenter bee | Round hole in bare wood, sawdust below | Males hover and bluff; females bore. A structural question, not a sting question |

## When a nest can be left alone

A paper wasp nest under a shed eave, away from doors and seating, at head height or above, does little harm and does useful work eating caterpillars. A mud dauber is not a threat to anybody. A nest found in late autumn is ending anyway.

Our default with a low-risk nest is to say so. The nests that do need dealing with are the ones near where people live: beside a door, over a deck, in the wall of a bedroom, in a play area, or anywhere in a lawn that gets mown — and any nest at all where someone in the household has a sting allergy.

## Why ground nests are the dangerous ones

Yellowjackets nesting in a lawn, a bank or a stone wall are triggered by vibration. A lawnmower, a wheelbarrow or a child running past produces a defensive response from a colony that may number in the thousands by August, and the entrance is often invisible until you are standing on it.

If you have found one: mark it from a safe distance, keep people and pets away, and do not attempt it in daylight with a retail can. That is when the foragers are out and returning, and it is how a single sting becomes a dozen.

## Late-summer scavenging

The yellowjackets at your picnic table in August are a different behaviour from nest defence. The colony's brood production has slowed and workers are foraging for sugar, which is why they suddenly find your drink interesting. Covering food and drinks, keeping bins closed and moving eating away from the bin does more than any treatment.

## What treatment involves

Location first: a nest is treated where it lives, not where the insects are seen. Timing matters — treatment at dusk or after dark, when the colony is inside, is both safer and more effective. Enclosed nests in wall voids need the void treated and then the entry sealed only after activity stops; sealing a live nest in a wall pushes the colony into the room on the other side of it.

Afterwards, the nest material is removed where it is reachable, and the entry point is closed so the site is not reused. For carpenter bees, the hole is treated and then plugged, and the bare wood that attracted them gets a recommendation to seal or paint it.
