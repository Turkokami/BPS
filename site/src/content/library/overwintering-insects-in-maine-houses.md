Every Maine winter produces the same call: it is February, the sun is out, and there are twenty insects at the window in an upstairs bedroom. Nothing is wrong with the house, nobody is unhygienic, and no infestation is under way. What is happening is that insects which moved into the building's walls in September have been woken up by a warm wall.

## Which one is it

| What you are seeing | Likely species | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow, slightly larger than a housefly, sluggish, clusters at windows | Cluster fly | Develops in soil outdoors; does not breed indoors |
| Shield-shaped, mottled brown, appears in numbers, smells when squashed | Brown marmorated stink bug | Agricultural pest outdoors; a nuisance indoors |
| Black with red or orange markings, on sunny walls in autumn | Boxelder bug | Associated with boxelder and maple |
| Orange to red, spotted, bites occasionally, stains when crushed | Asian lady beetle | Not the native ladybug most people picture |

None of these damages the structure, the food in your cupboards, or your family. They are a nuisance, and the honest answer to "what do I spray" is usually "nothing, indoors."

## Why they are in the wall in the first place

In September and October these insects look for a sheltered place to spend winter, and a heated building radiating warmth on a cool afternoon is the most attractive thing in the landscape. They gather on the sunny side — typically the south and west walls — and work into the first gap they find: around window frames, under lifted siding, behind fascia, through unscreened soffit and ridge vents, and around utility penetrations.

Once inside the void they go dormant. They are not eating, not breeding, and not moving. Then a warm spell in January, February or March raises the wall temperature enough to wake a few of them, and they move toward the light rather than back out the way they came. That is why it is always a window, and always in the middle of winter.

## What to do now, in February

1. Vacuum them. It is unglamorous and it is the correct answer. Empty the canister outside.
2. If they are persistent at one window, check the weatherstripping and the gap where the frame meets the wall — that is often the route from the void into the room.
3. Do not spray them. They are emerging from inside a void, so an application at the window treats the ones you can see and nothing else, for weeks.
4. Keep a note of which rooms and which walls. That is the map for the work in September.

## What to do in September, which is the actual fix

Exclusion, on the sunny side of the building first:

- Seal around window and door frames, and replace failed weatherstripping.
- Screen ridge, gable and soffit vents, and repair any screening that has rusted.
- Close the gaps around dryer vents, bath fan terminations, hose bibs, cable and electrical entries, and mini-split line sets.
- Repair lifted or damaged siding, fascia, and trim where a gap has opened.
- Where pressure is heavy, a timed exterior application in early autumn — before the insects settle in — reduces how many get in at all. That is licensed work and the timing window is narrow; applied in November it is a waste of money.

## The pattern worth noticing

Every one of those repairs is the same repair that keeps mice out, and the same one that stops carpenter ants finding wet wood. A Maine house tightened for one of these problems is tightened for all three, which is why exclusion is the recommendation this site keeps returning to. It is the only pest control work that stops costing money.
