Mosquitoes on a Maine property come from two places: water on the property itself, and adults arriving from elsewhere that then rest in the shaded, humid parts of the yard. The programme works on both, and the honest version is that the first one is where the leverage is.

## The water audit

Mosquito larvae develop in still water, and it takes very little. On a typical property the productive sites are:

- Saucers under planters, and the planters themselves
- A tarp, a wheelbarrow, a kayak, an upturned lid, a child's toy
- Clogged gutters, especially on the shaded side
- Corrugated downspout extensions that hold water in the ribs
- Tyres, buckets, and the rim of a rain barrel that is not screened
- A birdbath or pet bowl that is not emptied twice a week
- Low spots that hold water for more than a few days after rain
- Untended pool covers and unmaintained water features

We walk the property and list these, because a site that produces mosquitoes every week outperforms anything sprayed around it.

## Treatment, where it helps

Adults rest during the day in cool, humid, shaded vegetation: dense shrubs, the underside of foliage, ivy, the shaded fence line, tall grass at the edge. A targeted residual application to those resting sites reduces the population people encounter in the evening. Where standing water cannot be removed — a drainage swale, a catch basin, an ornamental pond — larvicide is the appropriate tool rather than more adult treatment.

Areas are chosen and areas are excluded. Flowering plants, vegetable gardens and pollinator habitat are named in the plan and avoided.

## What this cannot do

A yard programme will not make an evening on a Maine porch in July feel like an evening in January. Adults fly in from wetland, ditch and neighbouring property, and nothing applied inside your boundary changes that. What a programme reasonably does is cut what your own property produces and knock down what is resting in it, which for most households is the difference between unusable and pleasant.

## Licensing

Mosquito and biting-fly work sits in category 7E with the Maine Board of Pesticides Control, separate from structural pest control, and applicators may only treat in the categories they hold. The Board publishes a public directory of licensed commercial applicators.

## Timing an event

Where a treatment is for a specific date — a wedding, a graduation party, a weekend with family visiting — timing matters more than volume. A residual application a few days ahead, with the water sources cleared the week before, gives a far better evening than a heavy application the morning of. Tell us the date when you call and the plan is built backwards from it.

## What we record

The plan names the treated zones, the excluded zones, the water sources found and who is going to deal with each one. If the biggest producer on your property is a neighbour's uncovered rain barrel three metres over the line, the report says so, because you cannot fix what nobody has told you about.

## Why a mosquito problem is really a water problem

A mosquito needs still water to complete its life cycle, and the amount required is smaller than people expect: a saucer under a flowerpot, the fold of a tarp, the ribs of a corrugated downspout extension. The adults you swat in the evening developed somewhere within a short flight, and on most residential properties a meaningful share of them developed on the property itself.

That is the whole reason the walk-through comes before the sprayer. A site that produces a new generation every week or two will outrun any treatment schedule you buy, and the fix is usually free: tip it, screen it, drain it, or store it upside down.

## The order we work in

1. **Survey.** Walk the property and list every water-holding item and low spot, including the ones behind the shed that nobody looks at.
2. **Eliminate.** Anything that can be emptied, tipped, stored differently or drained gets fixed first, and the list goes to you in writing.
3. **Treat what cannot be eliminated.** A swale, a catch basin, an ornamental pond or a rain barrel that must stay gets a larvicide appropriate to that setting rather than an adult application.
4. **Reduce resting habitat.** Targeted residual application to the shaded, humid vegetation where adults rest during the day.
5. **Stage through the season.** Rain and heat drive the cycle, so timing follows conditions rather than a fixed date on an invoice.

## Ponds, rain barrels and chickens

Plenty of Maine properties have features that hold water on purpose. A rain barrel should be screened, not treated. A stocked pond with fish usually manages its own larvae and needs nothing. Chicken waterers and stock tanks are refreshed rather than treated. None of that is a reason to skip the property — it is a reason to have someone walk it who will tell you which features matter and which do not.

## Black flies, and being honest about them

Black flies breed in moving water — streams and brooks, often well beyond your property line — so nothing applied to your yard touches the source. Treatment reduces resting adults and that is all it does. In black fly season the practical measures are timing, clothing and repellent, and we would rather say so than sell a programme that cannot deliver.

## What you can do without hiring anyone

Empty or store anything that holds water. Screen the rain barrel. Clean the gutters. Replace corrugated downspout extensions with smooth ones. Refresh birdbaths and pet bowls twice a week. Fill the low spot that holds water for a week after rain. Thin dense shade near the patio.

That list costs a weekend and it will do more for July than anything sold by the application. We would rather tell you that and be called when it is not enough.

## Bookings and timing

Treatment is scheduled around rain and heat rather than a fixed calendar date, and we will move a visit rather than apply into weather that will wash it off. If you have a date that matters, say so when you book and the schedule is built backwards from it.

## What the report contains

Every water source found, with a note on who deals with it and how. The resting habitat we intend to treat, and the areas excluded — vegetable beds, flowering plants, ponds with fish, chicken runs. The treatment schedule for the season, with the two or three rounds that matter most flagged. And an honest note about anything on a neighbouring property that is driving the problem more than your own yard is.

## Questions worth asking any mosquito company

1. Will you walk the property for breeding sites before you treat?
2. What exactly are you applying, and where will it go?
3. What will you not treat, and how do I tell you what to avoid?
4. How does the schedule change when it rains?
5. What result am I actually buying — fewer mosquitoes, or none?

The last one is the one that separates honest programmes from oversold ones.
