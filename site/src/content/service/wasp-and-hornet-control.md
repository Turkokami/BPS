Maine's stinging-insect season is short and it escalates fast. A queen starts a nest in April or May, the colony is barely noticeable through June, and by August it is at maximum size and at its most defensive — which is exactly when people are outside using the deck.

## What is actually on your property

| Insect | Nest | How it behaves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paper wasp | Open, umbrella-shaped comb under eaves, rails and grills | Least aggressive; stings when the nest itself is disturbed |
| Yellowjacket | Enclosed, in the ground, in wall voids, in sheds | Most likely to sting in numbers; scavenges food and drink in late summer |
| Bald-faced hornet | Large grey paper nest, often in a tree or on a wall | Very defensive within a few metres of the nest |
| Mud dauber | Small mud tubes on walls and in sheds | Solitary, rarely stings; usually best left alone |
| Carpenter bee | Round hole in bare wood with sawdust below | Males bluff, females bore. A structural question rather than a sting question |

## Which nests we treat, and which we do not

A paper wasp nest under a shed eave, away from doors and seating, is doing more good than harm — those wasps spend the summer taking caterpillars off your garden. We will say so rather than sell you a treatment.

The nests that need dealing with are the ones near where people live: beside a door, over a deck or patio, in a bedroom wall, in a play area, anywhere in a lawn that gets mown, and any nest at all where somebody in the household has a sting allergy.

Nests found in late October are usually already collapsing with the cold. We will tell you that too.

## Ground nests are the dangerous ones

Yellowjackets nesting in a lawn, a bank or a stone wall are triggered by vibration. A mower, a wheelbarrow or a child running past sets off a defensive response from a colony that can number in the thousands by August, and the entrance is often invisible until somebody is standing on it.

If you have found one: mark it from a safe distance, keep people and pets away, and do not try it in daylight with a retail can. Daylight is when the foragers are out and returning, and that is how one sting becomes a dozen.

## How a visit works

1. Locate the nest — not the insects. Wasps forage a long way from home, and the treatment has to reach the colony.
2. Identify the species, because a bald-faced hornet nest, a ground yellowjacket colony and a paper wasp comb are three different jobs.
3. Treat at dusk or after dark where possible, when the colony is inside and the risk is lowest.
4. Leave the entry open until activity stops, then seal it so the site is not reused.
5. Remove the nest material where it is reachable, and note anything structural that made the spot attractive.

## Late-summer scavenging is a different problem

The yellowjackets at your picnic table in August are not defending anything — the colony's brood production has slowed and workers are out looking for sugar. Covering food and drinks, keeping bin lids closed and moving the eating area away from the bin does more than any treatment will.

## Carpenter bees

Round holes in bare fascia, rails or shed framing, with coarse sawdust below. The gallery is treated, then plugged, and the bare wood that attracted them gets a recommendation to seal or paint. Treating without dealing with the timber gets you the same holes a season later.

## What it costs

Nest work is quoted per nest, and the price depends on access and height far more than on species — a nest reachable from the ground is straightforward, one in a third-storey gable is not. Where there are several nests, or a property that produces them every year, a programme is usually cheaper than repeat call-outs, and we will say which of the two your property actually needs.

## Carpenter bees

Round holes about the diameter of a finger in bare fascia, rails, deck framing or shed timber, with coarse sawdust below and a male hovering nearby doing a convincing impression of a threat. The males cannot sting. The females bore, and they return to the same timber year after year, extending the same galleries.

The work is: treat the gallery, then plug it once activity has stopped, then deal with the bare wood that attracted them. Treating without sealing and finishing the timber gets you the same holes a season later, slightly bigger.

Woodpeckers make this worse by opening galleries to get at the larvae, which turns a tidy hole into a torn one. If a fascia board has been worked over by both, it is a carpentry repair with a pest control step in front of it, and the report will say so.
