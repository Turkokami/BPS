Commercial work is judged on two things a homeowner never thinks about: whether the paperwork holds up, and whether the service disrupts trading. Both are scheduling and documentation problems as much as pest control problems.

## Who this is for

- Restaurants, cafés, bakeries and other food premises
- Apartment buildings and multi-unit rentals
- Offices and retail units
- Warehouses and storage facilities
- Property managers running several buildings across the Lewiston–Auburn corridor and beyond

## What a commercial programme includes

1. A baseline inspection of the interior, the exterior, the waste area and the deliveries route, with the conducive conditions written down.
2. A service schedule that fits your hours, not ours.
3. A written record for every visit: areas inspected, evidence found, materials applied and where, recommendations outstanding.
4. Monitoring devices where they are useful, placed and numbered so trends over time mean something.
5. A named contact — the same person each visit.

## Multi-unit buildings

Bed bugs and German cockroaches move through wall voids, pipe chases and corridors. A programme that treats only the unit that complained produces a result that lasts until the population next door reaches the same threshold. Scoping the neighbouring units at the start is cheaper than treating the whole floor twice.

Where access is the constraint — and it usually is — the plan documents what was reachable and what was not, so a manager can act on it.

## Maine's notice and posting rules

The rule that does most to shape commercial and multi-unit work in this state is Chapter 26 of the Maine Board of Pesticides Control's rules, "Standards for Indoor Pesticide Applications and Notification for All Occupied Buildings Except K-12 Schools". The Board says it applies to governmental, commercial and institutional buildings, condominiums, rented residential buildings, and licensed childcare facilities and nursery schools. In outline, from the rule text:

- **Businesses and agencies.** The Board-approved written notice must be posted where employee notices usually go, at least 24 hours and no more than seven days before an application, and stay up for at least 48 hours afterwards.
- **Rented homes and condominiums.** Residents of a unit being treated at someone else's request get the notice individually in the same 24-hour-to-seven-day window; for common areas it is posted and left up for 48 hours after.
- **Information on request.** Anyone notified may ask for the product's trade name and EPA registration number, the date, time and location, the label's re-entry interval, and a contact name and number, and the applicator must supply them.
- **Tenant consent.** A tenant's unit may not be treated over their objection unless a public health or code enforcement official has found an immediate need.
- **Exemptions.** Baits, gels, pastes, dusts and granular products placed where occupants cannot readily reach them, and crack-and-crevice treatments done to minimise vapours, are exempt from the notice steps.

The applicator can hand the notice to the building manager to post or distribute, but must confirm it was done before treating. On a multi-unit job that confirmation is part of the schedule, which is one more reason the first visit is a planning visit.

## What Chapter 26 asks of the applicator

The same rule requires commercial applicators working inside these buildings to use integrated pest management, identify the pest and how bad the infestation is before applying anything, and give the client a written evaluation of the conditions that encourage pests along with specific, practical non-pesticide recommendations. Chapter 26 also limits what can be used while people are in the room: unless the label prohibits it, only baits, gels, pastes, granules and crack-and-crevice treatments. The rule's own definition of IPM says applications are not made to a pre-determined schedule but only where monitoring or history shows they are needed. The written conditions report described above is not a courtesy; for these buildings it is part of the rule.

## Licensing and insurance

The Maine Board of Pesticides Control requires a firm licence for custom applicator companies other than sole proprietors with no employees. That licence runs $300 for three years, and requires both proof of insurance and a designated Commercial Master applicator who carries responsibility for pest management decisions and employee training. Any commercial customer is entitled to ask a contractor for those details, and to check the Board's public directory of licensed commercial applicators.

## The site survey

A commercial survey that produces a usable scope takes real time: the size of the property, the documentation or reporting the site needs and the pest involved all change how long it runs. Ask about the survey when you call. What comes out of it is a written scope you can compare line by line against anyone else's.

## What an inspector is actually looking for

Commercial pest control is judged on evidence of a system. In practice that means:

- A current service record on site, not in a filing cabinet somewhere else
- A site plan showing monitoring devices, numbered and located
- A trend over time rather than a stack of identical visit sheets
- Evidence that recommendations were acted on and closed out
- Product records: what was applied, where, in what category, by whom

A file showing the same open recommendation month after month is worse than no file, because it documents that the business knew and did nothing. We write recommendations so they can be closed, and we record when they are.

## Restaurants and food premises

Food premises get the same inspection discipline with tighter constraints: service outside trading hours, no product on or near food-contact surfaces, monitoring placed where it will not be disturbed by cleaning, and particular attention to the three places problems start — the waste area, the delivery route, and the void behind and beneath fixed equipment.

The conducive conditions that cause most restaurant pest problems are structural or procedural rather than biological: a door propped open in summer, a gap under a delivery door, cardboard stored against a wall, a floor drain that is never cleaned, a grease trap serviced late. Those go in the report, because they are cheaper to fix than to treat around forever.

## Rentals and property managers

For a landlord or a manager, the hard part is not treatment, it is access and sequencing. A building where units are treated one at a time as complaints arrive will treat the same infestation repeatedly. A building where a cluster is inspected and treated in one window will not.

We will schedule to a block of units, document which were not reachable, and re-book them rather than quietly skipping them, so the reason a building is still seeing activity is visible rather than mysterious.

## Reporting to head office

Where a site is one of several, we will keep the reporting in a format that can be compared across sites: same fields, same device numbering scheme, same recommendation language. That matters more than it sounds. A manager comparing four buildings needs the records to be legible side by side.

## Frequency, and how to pick it

Monthly is the default for food premises for a reason: it matches how fast a population can establish between visits and it produces enough data points for a trend. Quarterly suits low-risk offices and retail. Weekly or fortnightly belongs to a building working through an active infestation, and it should have an end date rather than becoming the permanent arrangement.

We will recommend the lowest frequency that fits the risk, and say plainly when a site is being serviced more often than it needs.

## What we need from you

Access, a named point of contact, somewhere to keep the record, and a route for recommendations to reach whoever can act on them. Most commercial programmes that fail, fail on one of those four rather than on anything technical.

## Multi-site and seasonal work

For operators running several buildings, or a seasonal site such as a campground or a summer rental block, the plan is built around the calendar the business actually runs on: opening inspection, in-season service at the agreed frequency, closing inspection and a written condition report for the off-season. Records carry across years so the same recurring problem is visible rather than rediscovered.

## What we ask before quoting

Square footage and layout, what the premises does, the hours it trades, the pest history, who holds keys and access, and whether the building shares walls, ventilation or waste facilities with anyone else. A quote without those is a guess that gets revised upward, and revised quotes are how commercial relationships start badly.
