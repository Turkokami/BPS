Mice move indoors in Maine when the nights start dropping, and they do not need much of a gap to do it. A mouse follows a warm draft to its source, and the gaps that produce those drafts are the same ones an older Maine house already has: the gap where the sill meets the foundation, the corner where a soffit return dies into the roofline, the hole a plumber cut for a pipe and never closed, the door sweep that has worn back a quarter inch.

## What the inspection covers

The job starts outside. Rodent work that starts with a trap in the kitchen is solving the symptom in the room where you noticed it, and the animals will keep arriving from wherever they were arriving from.

| Where we look | What we are looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Foundation and sill | Gaps at the sill plate, utility penetrations, failed mortar, bulkhead seals |
| Roofline and soffits | Open returns, lifted drip edge, gaps behind gutters, vent screens that have rusted through |
| Attic and crawlspace | Droppings, runways, gnawed wiring, insulation compressed into travel paths |
| Kitchen and utility rooms | Entry behind cabinets and appliances, dishwasher and range lines, pantry damage |
| Exterior grounds | Feeders, pet food, compost, woodpiles and any structure connecting to the house |

## Removal, then exclusion

Once the route is known, removal is the fast part. Traps go where the evidence is — along runways, against walls, in the void the animals are actually using, not where it is convenient to reach. Traps and bait go in tamper-resistant stations or out of reach of children and pets, as the label requires.

Exclusion is the part that decides whether you call anyone again. Sealing is a material job: copper mesh and sealant in small gaps, hardware cloth over vents and larger openings, and metal where a rodent has already chewed something soft. A foam-only repair fails, because a mouse chews foam.

## Mice and rats are not the same job

Maine homes see house mice, deer mice and, in the older housing stock and near commercial waste, Norway rats. The distinction matters because it changes where the work happens.

A mouse needs a gap about the width of a pencil. It nests close to a food source, usually inside the structure itself — in insulation, behind a knee wall, inside a cabinet kick space — and a single female can produce litters through the winter once she is somewhere warm. Mouse work is mostly interior voids and small exterior gaps.

A Norway rat is a bigger animal with a bigger appetite for structure. It burrows, it travels along foundations and sewer lines, and it chews openings rather than finding them. Rat work moves outward: burrows along the foundation, the bulkhead, the crawlspace vents, the gap under a deck or shed, the drain line that was capped with something soft. Rat pressure in a neighborhood is rarely one property's problem, and the honest version of the conversation includes what your neighbors' bins and feeders are contributing.

Deer mice matter for a different reason. They are the species most associated with hantavirus in the United States, which is why we do not sweep or vacuum droppings dry in an attic or crawlspace, and why a cleanup recommendation comes with ventilation and wetting instructions rather than a broom.

## What happens on the first visit

1. Walk the exterior first, at ground level and at the roofline, and mark every gap that a rodent could use.
2. Walk the interior where the activity was reported, then the spaces above and below it, because the room you hear them in is usually not the room they live in.
3. Identify the species from droppings, gnaw marks, runways and grease trails rather than from what the animal looked like when it ran.
4. Place removal devices along the confirmed routes, out of reach of children and pets.
5. Write the exclusion quote with each opening listed separately, and flag anything that belongs to a carpenter or a roofer rather than to us.

## Why the sealing work gets quoted with the trapping

A trapping-only program produces a quiet house and a standing invoice. The animals inside are removed, the openings stay open, and the population outside refills the space on its own schedule — which in Maine is usually the first hard cold of the next fall. Every visit after that is billed.

Sealing costs more the first time and less every year after. It is also the part of the job that survives a change of pest control company, because a sealed soffit return stays sealed whoever you call next.

## Attic and insulation damage

Once rodents have been in an attic through a winter, the insulation in the travel paths is compressed, soiled, and no longer doing its job. Not every attic needs to be stripped, and we will say so when it does not. When it does, the sequence is: exclude first, remove the animals, then remediate — because new insulation installed while the entry points are open is new insulation with a mouse runway through it by spring.

## How the program runs

A rodent job here is a full inspection, sealing of the entry points found, and a separate quote for anything that needs more serious repair. Ongoing cover runs monthly or quarterly depending on the property and the pressure, and the point of it is that the exclusion list gets shorter every year rather than the visits getting more frequent.

## What this costs and how we quote it

Inspections and quotes are free, and the quote separates the treatment from the sealing so you can see what you are paying for. If a job needs a contractor rather than a pest control license — a rotted sill, a roof repair — we say so rather than sealing over it.

## What we check before quoting exclusion

Sealing a house is not one price per house. What it costs depends on how many openings there are, how high they are, and whether the material around them is sound. The survey covers six things, and the quote lists each one separately:

1. **Ground level.** Sill plate gaps, foundation cracks wide enough to admit a mouse, bulkhead seals, crawlspace vents, and anywhere a utility line enters the building.
2. **Doors.** Garage door bottom seals and side seals are the single most common entry on a Maine house with an attached garage, and they are cheap to correct.
3. **Rooflines.** Soffit returns, the joint where a roof plane meets a wall, lifted drip edge, gable and ridge vents.
4. **Penetrations.** Dryer vents, bath fan terminations, hose bibs, mini-split line sets, radon fan piping, electrical service entry.
5. **Material condition.** A rotted sill or a soft fascia board cannot hold a repair. That is named as carpentry, quoted by someone else, and we say so before you spend money sealing it.
6. **Ongoing pressure.** Woodpiles against the foundation, feeders, compost, a shed with a gap under it, a neighboring barn.

## Winter work versus fall work

The cheapest rodent job is the one done in September. The most expensive is the one done in January, because by then the animals are established, the insulation is already soiled, and half the exterior is under snow and ice where sealing work cannot be done properly.

If you are reading this in the middle of winter with activity overhead, the sequence changes: remove first, seal what is reachable, and diary the rest of the exterior work for the thaw. We will tell you which items we can do now and which will hold until spring rather than charging for a repair that will not last.

## What we leave you with

A written list of every opening found and what it needs, the removal plan, the sealing quote item by item, and anything that belongs to another trade. If a second company quotes the same house, you should be able to lay the two quotes side by side and compare them line for line. That is the point of itemizing it.

## Working inside the rules

Rodent work done for a fee in Maine is licensed pest control work, and it is worth asking any company you call whether it is licensed and insured.

Rodent pressure in a Maine house is seasonal but the damage is not: gnawed wiring, contaminated insulation, and droppings in a space you store things in are all cumulative. The point of doing it properly once is that you stop paying for it every fall.
