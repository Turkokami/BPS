Rodent damage in an attic is cumulative and invisible until somebody goes up there. The insulation loses its loft where animals have been travelling, droppings work down through the bays, and any exposed wiring is a question worth answering before anything else.

## What the assessment covers

- Which bays are contaminated and which are intact
- Depth and condition of the remaining insulation, and whether it is still doing its job
- Droppings and nesting material through the space and in stored items
- Gnawing on wiring, ducting, and any plastic or soft pipework
- Whether the roofline and the rest of the structure have actually been sealed
- Ventilation — soffit and ridge airflow, which is often blocked by displaced insulation

## Signs the attic has been occupied

Most people find out from a noise at night, but the attic usually tells you more than the ceiling does. University of California IPM says droppings and gnaw marks are what reveal house mouse activity, and that a characteristic musky odour is common where an infestation is large or long-standing. Add to that the things you can see from the hatch with a torch: insulation flattened into paths along the eaves, shredded paper or fabric pushed into a corner, and dark smudges where animals squeeze past the same rafter every night.

If you see any of those, look from the hatch and stop there. Climbing in to investigate is exactly the dry disturbance the precautions below are written to avoid.

## Why droppings in a roof space are treated carefully

The U.S. CDC says people get hantavirus from contact with rodents such as rats and mice, especially through exposure to their urine, droppings and saliva, and it names the deer mouse as the most common carrier in the United States. The virus it carries, Sin Nombre, causes hantavirus pulmonary syndrome. The CDC's figures are sobering: symptoms usually appear one to eight weeks after contact with an infected rodent, and 38 per cent of people who develop respiratory symptoms may die.

That is not a problem confined to other states. The UMaine Animal Health Laboratory reported the first diagnosed human case in Maine in 2011, and its director's advice to Maine residents was to keep rodents out of the house, barn or camp and, when cleaning up after them, not to breathe the dust.

The risk in an attic is not touching a mouse. It is stirring up dried droppings and nesting material in an enclosed space and breathing what comes off them. Sweeping and vacuuming are the two quickest ways to do that, which is why neither has any place in a rodent clean-up. The CDC also notes that the one hantavirus known to pass from person to person is not found in the United States, so the concern is the space, not the household.

## The CDC's clean-up method, for small jobs you do yourself

A few droppings on a shelf or along one stretch of the attic floor are a reasonable thing to deal with yourself, provided you do it the way the U.S. CDC sets out:

1. **Ventilate first.** Open doors and windows for 30 minutes before you start, and stay out of the space while it airs.
2. **Glove up.** Wear rubber or plastic gloves. University of California IPM specifies non-fabric gloves — rubber, latex, vinyl or nitrile.
3. **Mix the solution.** The CDC's ratio is 1.5 cups of household bleach to a gallon of water, which is one part bleach to nine parts water. An EPA-registered disinfectant used to its label is the alternative.
4. **Soak, do not scrub.** Spray the droppings and urine until very wet and leave them for 5 minutes, or for the time on the disinfectant label.
5. **Wipe and bin.** Pick the material up with paper towels and put them in a covered rubbish bin that is emptied regularly.
6. **Finish the surface.** Mop or sponge the area with disinfectant.
7. **Wash.** Remove the gloves and wash your hands with soap and warm water.

The CDC's warning is the line to remember: do not vacuum or sweep rodent urine, droppings or nesting material, because it can put tiny droplets containing virus into the air. The UMaine Animal Health Laboratory adds a mask before entering a space that may be contaminated.

Where the job is bigger than that — droppings through most of the bays, nesting in the insulation, a space that has held animals for more than one season — the CDC's own advice for heavy infestations is to bring in a professional rather than tackle it as a household chore.

## The order of work, and why it is not negotiable

1. **Exclude.** Every opening found is closed, with materials that hold. Until this is done, nothing else is worth paying for.
2. **Remove.** The animals inside come out first, and activity is confirmed to have stopped.
3. **Clean out.** Contaminated insulation and nesting material are removed damp, bagged in the space, and taken away rather than carried loose through the house.
4. **Sanitise.** The affected surfaces are treated, and the space ventilated.
5. **Re-insulate.** New material goes in last, to the depth the space actually needs, with baffles kept clear.

## Getting the insulation depth right

Clearing out soiled insulation is also the one time the full depth of an attic floor is easy to reset. ENERGY STAR's retrofit guidance for climate zones 6 and 7 recommends R60 for an uninsulated attic, or R49 where three to four inches of insulation already exist. The Maine State Fire Marshal's guide to the state's energy code places Maine in those two zones.

Those are recommendations, not a sales target. What goes back is matched to what the space had, what the household wants, and what ventilation the roof needs — and if the bays were never insulated to that depth in the first place, you will be told so rather than surprised by it.

## Deer mice or house mice

Both use attics, and the clean-up is the same either way. Identification still matters, because deer mice are the hantavirus concern. University of California IPM gives the clearest field mark: a bicoloured tail, dark above and white below, along with white feet and underparts and larger eyes and ears than a house mouse. The same source notes that deer mice often move into vacated homes, cabins and other structures to nest and store food, which is why a camp or a seasonal cottage opened up in spring deserves the full CDC routine before anyone starts sweeping.

## What you get afterwards

A record of what was removed, what was treated, what was installed and to what depth, plus photographs of the space before and after. If ventilation or wiring needs a different trade, that is named rather than left for you to discover.

## When we will tell you not to bother

A light scattering of droppings in a corner of an otherwise sound attic does not justify a clean-out. Neither does a single season of low-level activity in a space with good insulation. In both cases the money is better spent on the sealing that stops it happening again, and we would rather say that than sell the bigger job. The CDC method above is the right answer for that kind of attic, and it costs you a bottle of bleach.
