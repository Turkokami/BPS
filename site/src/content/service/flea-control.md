Fleas are one of the few pest problems where the most important step is not ours. The animal is treated by a vet; the building is treated by us; and the two have to happen close together or the cycle simply restarts.

## Why it takes more than one visit

A flea population in a house is mostly not adults. The eggs fall off the animal and collect wherever it sleeps and walks — carpet fibres, upholstery, the gaps between floorboards, pet bedding. Larvae develop down in that material, and the pupal stage is protected by a cocoon that most treatments cannot reach.

Those pupae hatch in response to warmth, vibration and carbon dioxide, which means they hatch when you walk past. So seeing fleas after a treatment is the expected pattern rather than a failure, and the follow-up visit is what catches the emerging generation.

## Preparation that actually matters

1. Vacuum everywhere the pet goes, including upholstery and along the skirting, then empty the canister into an outside bin immediately.
2. Wash pet bedding on the hottest cycle the fabric allows.
3. Clear floors so that treatable surfaces can actually be reached.
4. Book the vet in the same week — a treated environment with an untreated animal is a short-lived result.
5. Keep vacuuming after treatment. It brings pupae out of the cocoon and into contact with what has been applied.

## What the visit covers

Interior treatment concentrated where the animal rests and travels rather than applied uniformly across every room, the pet's bedding areas, and the entry points and crawlspaces where wildlife may be maintaining a population outside. Where a crawlspace or a deck void is the real source, that gets addressed rather than treated around.

## Pets, children and product placement

Tell us what lives in the house, including fish tanks and birds, and where children play. Placement and timing change accordingly, and any area we are leaving alone is written into the plan before anything is applied.

## Fleas without a pet

A house with no animals can still produce fleas: a previous owner's pet, a raccoon or squirrel in the attic, or feral cats under a deck. In those cases the treatment is a holding action until the wildlife route is closed — and closing that route is exclusion work rather than flea work, which we will quote separately and honestly.
