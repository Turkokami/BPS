Fleas are one of the few pest problems where the most important step is not ours. The animal is treated by a vet; the building is treated by us; and the two have to happen close together or the cycle simply restarts.

## Which flea, and how it lives

UMaine Cooperative Extension says the flea most often found in Maine homes is the cat flea, and that it passes through four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult. Only the last of those lives on the animal. The rest of the population is in the house, and that is the part a pet product alone does not reach.

The same Extension fact sheet sets out the timing. Eggs hatch in one to twelve days. The larvae — eyeless, legless and pale — work their way into floor cracks, carpet and pet beds, moult three times, and mature in seven to 15 days. After roughly a week in the cocoon, the adults are ready to emerge. UMaine puts the full cycle at 27 to 40 days, which is why a flea problem that seemed to arrive overnight has usually been building for a month.

What the larvae eat matters for the clean-up. UMaine Cooperative Extension lists food crumbs, animal waste and the droppings of adult fleas, so the material a larva lives on is exactly what gathers in a pet's favourite sleeping spot. Thorough vacuuming takes away both the larvae and their food.

## Why it takes more than one visit

The eggs fall off the animal and collect wherever it sleeps and walks — carpet fibres, upholstery, the gaps between floorboards, pet bedding. The pupal stage sits inside a cocoon, and no treatment applied on a single day can be relied on to reach every one of them.

Those adults do not leave the cocoon on a timetable. UMaine Cooperative Extension says they can rest inside until a noise or vibration indicates an animal or a person nearby, and the U.S. CDC says fleas find hosts by detecting body heat, movement, vibration and breathing. In practice that means they hatch when you walk past. Seeing fleas after a treatment is the expected pattern rather than a failure, and the follow-up visit is what catches the emerging generation.

## Signs worth checking before you call

- **Bites low on the body.** UMaine Cooperative Extension says flea bites are most likely on the feet, ankles and legs, often a small red spot with a red halo, sometimes swollen.
- **A pet scratching at the base of the tail or the neck.** Your vet can confirm fleas on the animal quickly, and that confirmation is worth having before anyone treats the house.
- **Dark specks in the pet's bedding.** Adult flea droppings are the larvae's food, so where they collect is where the larvae are.
- **Fleas appearing after a house has stood empty.** UMaine Cooperative Extension notes that severe flea problems often show up after several weeks of vacation or when moving into an empty house.

If you have bites but no pet and no sightings, say so on the phone. Other things bite, and naming the insect comes before treating for it.

## What you can do yourself

Most of the work in a flea job is the household's, and the sources agree on what helps.

- **Treat the animal.** The U.S. CDC recommends talking to your veterinarian about the right flea product for your pet and treating year-round, so adults are killed and new ones are prevented from hatching. UMaine Cooperative Extension is equally direct: pets must be treated with a product from the pet shop or the vet.
- **Vacuum, and get rid of what you collect.** The CDC advises vacuuming carpets, rugs and the cushions on chairs and sofas well and often, and emptying the vacuum bag outside. UMaine Cooperative Extension adds that sealing the bag inside a plastic bag before disposal avoids spreading the fleas.
- **Wash the bedding.** The CDC says to clean bedding, pet bedding especially, with soap and water frequently.
- **Close the wildlife route.** The CDC's flea prevention advice includes sealing holes where rodents can get into the home, storing pet food in tight containers, and clearing brush, junk and cluttered firewood from around the house.
- **Keep the yard less inviting.** The CDC suggests mowing often, because fleas avoid sun-exposed soil, and not over-watering, because they do well in humid ground.

None of that replaces treating the house when the population is established. All of it makes the treatment last longer.

## Preparation that actually matters

1. Vacuum everywhere the pet goes, including upholstery and along the skirting, then empty the canister into an outside bin immediately.
2. Wash pet bedding on the hottest cycle the fabric allows.
3. Clear floors so that treatable surfaces can actually be reached.
4. Book the vet in the same week — a treated environment with an untreated animal is a short-lived result.
5. Keep vacuuming after treatment. It brings pupae out of the cocoon and into contact with what has been applied.

## Pets, children and product placement

Tell us what lives in the house, including fish tanks and birds, and where children play. Placement and timing change accordingly, and any area we are leaving alone is written into the plan before anything is applied.

Children are worth naming for a second reason. The U.S. CDC says small children face a higher risk than adults of picking up flea-borne tapeworm, because they spend more time near the floor and on the carpet where fleas are found, and most people carrying one show no symptoms. If a child in the house has been unwell, that is a question for your doctor, not for us — but it is a good reason not to let a flea problem run.

## Fleas and health in Maine

The U.S. CDC lists the illnesses fleas can carry to people: flea-borne typhus, plague, cat scratch disease and tapeworm. That list reads worse than the local picture. UMaine Cooperative Extension says that although fleas are capable of transmitting diseases, this is rare in Maine, and the CDC notes that most U.S. cases of murine typhus are reported from California, Texas and Hawaii.

For a Maine household the realistic harm is itching, broken sleep, a miserable animal and a problem that grows for a month before anybody names it. That is reason enough to deal with it properly, and it is not a reason to panic.

## Fleas without a pet

A house with no animals can still produce fleas: a previous owner's pet, a raccoon or squirrel in the attic, or feral cats under a deck. In those cases the treatment is a holding action until the wildlife route is closed — and closing that route is exclusion work rather than flea work, which we will quote separately and honestly. ## Outside the house

UMaine Cooperative Extension observes that sandy soil and gravel driveways make good breeding ground for flea larvae, and that outdoor treatment, where it is needed, should extend 20 feet beyond the areas people and pets use. Whether the outside is part of your problem is something the visit establishes from where the animal spends its time, rather than an assumption made on the phone.
