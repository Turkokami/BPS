Carpenter ants are among the most commonly half-treated pests in a Maine house. The difference between an ant problem that ends and one that repeats every May is almost never the product. It is whether anyone worked out where the colony actually lives and what made that spot attractive.

## What carpenter ants are, in Maine

UMaine Cooperative Extension describes carpenter ants (genus *Camponotus*) as large ants named for their habit of excavating, tunnelling and living in wood, both indoors and out. The Extension lists the eastern black carpenter ant as the most common species in Maine, with the New York carpenter ant and a much smaller species, the smaller carpenter ant, whose workers are only about 5 mm long.

Size varies inside one colony too. The Maine Department of Agriculture's school IPM programme gives worker lengths from 1/4 inch to 5/8 inch and points to an arch-shaped thorax as a feature every carpenter ant shares. UMaine's fact sheet notes the smallest workers are about 1/4 inch long.

The point that matters for a house is their diet. UMaine Cooperative Extension says carpenter ants do not feed on wood the way termites do; they tunnel through it, and wet or rotting wood is particularly inviting. In the forest that makes them useful recyclers of dead timber. In a sill plate or a window frame it makes them a symptom of water.

Penn State Extension supports the winter reading: carpenter ants are active through the cold months when the nest is in a heated part of a building. The University of New Hampshire Extension adds that outdoor nests are inactive in winter, though individuals occasionally come out on warm days — so a single ant on a mild February afternoon is less telling than a steady stream of them.

## Carpenter ant or termite?

This is the question people most often ask after finding winged insects at a window, and the answer changes who you should call. Penn State Extension gives three features to check on a winged specimen:

- **Waist.** A carpenter ant's body is pinched in between the thorax and the abdomen. A termite's is not.
- **Antennae.** Carpenter ant antennae are elbowed. Termite antennae are straight, with bead-like segments.
- **Wings.** On a winged carpenter ant the front wings are longer than the hind wings. A termite's front and hind wings are about equal in length.

The damage differs as well. The Maine Department of Agriculture's school IPM fact sheet says carpenter ant tunnels cross the grain of the wood and are clean, while termite tunnels follow the grain and are covered with mud; carpenter ants also leave piles of frass outside their holes, which can contain wood fragments and pieces of dead insects.

## Signs worth checking before you call

UMaine Cooperative Extension says the presence of large ants is usually the first sign, coarse sawdust is a sure sign, and tapping on a suspect area may produce a hollow sound. Penn State Extension describes the debris as coarse, stringy wood particles mixed with dead insect parts.

The school IPM programme suggests checking trees and stumps near the building at night, when the ants are most active, and looking for small piles of wood particles or dead ants around attics, window sills and frames, porches, sinks, dishwashers and foundation sills. If you find a pile, sweep it up and look again a day later. Fresh frass in a spot you have just cleaned is the most useful thing you can show us.

## Where the water usually is

1. Window and door seals that have failed
2. A gutter line overflowing onto a sill or corner board
3. Framing behind a shower or tub surround
4. A porch post or deck ledger sitting in ground or standing water
5. Roof deck under an old ice-dam leak
6. A crawlspace with no vapour barrier

That list lines up with the authorities. UMaine Cooperative Extension's control advice starts with eliminating moisture from leaks around chimneys, roofs, gutters and windows, and the Maine school IPM programme says nests in buildings are usually associated with moisture problems such as roof or plumbing leaks. Every condition on the list is named and photographed in the report. Where the repair is carpentry or roofing rather than pest control, we say so instead of treating around it.

## Satellite colonies, and why the exterior walk matters

Penn State Extension describes the usual arrangement: a parent colony outside, typically within about 100 yards of the home, feeding satellite colonies inside it, and both have to be dealt with to prevent the problem returning. The parent — the one with the queen — tends to be in a stump, a dead limb, a woodpile or a section of fence. The satellite is what ends up in your wall, your window frame or your porch column.

So the inspection covers the tree line, any wood in ground contact, stumps within foraging distance, and branches touching the roof, before anything is quoted.

## What a treatment visit looks like

- Confirm the species from the insect rather than the description
- Follow the trail rather than the sighting — foragers travel along framing, wiring and pipe runs
- Treat the exterior of the structure where the entry and activity are
- Gel bait and traps inside, placed in the harbourages rather than smeared along baseboards
- Inject the galleries directly where they can be reached
- Write down the conditions keeping the house attractive

The University of New Hampshire Extension makes the same point about where the effort goes: it is essential to locate the nest and apply control there. The school IPM programme puts it more bluntly — locating and removing or treating the nest is the key, and is usually a job for a professional.

## What you can do yourself

- **Fix the water you already know about.** A dripping outside tap, a gutter that overflows onto trim, a window that weeps in a storm.
- **Move the woodpile.** UMaine Cooperative Extension recommends keeping firewood outside and checking it before bringing it in.
- **Cut back contact points.** The school IPM programme advises trimming plants so they do not touch the building, and keeping screens, weather stripping, door sweeps and gutters in good repair.
- **Vacuum the strays.** The same programme suggests vacuuming winged ants and small numbers of workers indoors.
- **Be careful with shop baits.** UMaine's fact sheet says baits should be labelled for carpenter ants and placed out of reach of children and pets. A bait that kills foragers without reaching the nest can make the colony harder to find.

## After treatment

Expect a few days of increased activity as the colony is disturbed and foragers redistribute. That is normal. Wipe away the frass after the visit so that new frass means something, and judge the result on the two to three weeks that follow rather than the first afternoon.

## The seasonal shape of it in Maine

Activity indoors in February or March means an interior colony. Penn State Extension says swarmers emerge from established colonies on warm days in spring and early summer, and the University of New Hampshire Extension gives spring and summer for the winged males and females. Those swarms are how new colonies start, and UMaine Cooperative Extension notes that any wet, rotten wood attracts a new queen. Midsummer is foraging season, when a kitchen trail is usually an exterior problem. By September the colony is provisioning rather than expanding, and the treatment timing shifts with it.

UMaine's fact sheet also says a well-fed colony may take two to five years to produce winged reproductives. Swarmers inside the house are therefore not the start of the problem; they are evidence of a colony that has been there for a while. That calendar is why a single spray in June often looks like a success and reads like a failure the following spring.
