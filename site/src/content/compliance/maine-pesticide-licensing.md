Pest control in Maine is a licensed trade, and the rules are public. This page exists so a homeowner can ask the right questions of any company — including this one.

## The licenses involved

| License | Who needs it | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commercial Applicator — Operator | Technicians applying pesticides under supervision | $105, three years, six recertification credits |
| Commercial Applicator — Master | One person per company, organization or branch office, responsible for pest management decisions, policy and training | $105, three years, nine recertification credits |
| Firm license | Custom applicator companies other than sole proprietors with no employees, or any incorporated company (Chapter 31) | $300, three years, plus proof of insurance |

The Board is explicit that an Operator license is only in effect while the employing company has at least one licensed Master applicator, and that a company may not operate without a responsible Master.

## The categories

A Maine commercial license is issued by category, and an applicator may only treat in the categories they hold. The ones that matter for a house are:

- **7A — Structural General Pest Control**, including food processing. Rodents, ants, cockroaches, bed bugs, most of what happens inside a building.
- **7E — Biting Fly and Other Arthropod Vectors.** Ticks and mosquitoes.
- **7F — Termite Pests.**
- **3A and 3B — Outdoor Ornamentals and Turf**, for lawn and shrub work.

The Board notes that applicators have been found reporting applications in areas they are not licensed for — weed control on a driveway, for instance, requires category 6B rather than a turf license. It is a reasonable question to ask.

## Insurance

Applicators who spray for hire must carry liability insurance whenever they make an application — at least $100,000 per person and $300,000 per occurrence, plus $100,000 property damage — and attest to it on the Board's form when they license (Chapter 31). "Licensed and insured" as a phrase on a website means very little; the license number and the category list mean something specific.

## How to check any company, including us

1. Ask for the applicator license number and the categories held.
2. Ask who the responsible Commercial Master applicator is.
3. Ask whether the company holds a firm license, or is a sole proprietor exempt from it.
4. Check the answer against the Board's own lists: [Licensed Commercial Applicators](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/php/pesticides/licensed-commericial-applicators.shtml) is searchable by applicator name, license number, expiry date and category, and [For Hire Pesticide Application Companies](https://www1.maine.gov/dacf/php/pesticides/pesticide-application-companies.shtml) lists every for-hire company with currently licensed commercial applicators, with its county and categories.
5. Ask what will be applied, in what category, and where.

## Why any of this matters to a homeowner

Three practical reasons, none of them abstract.

**Liability.** A licensed for-hire applicator must have liability insurance in force when applying; an unlicensed one has no such obligation, so if something goes wrong — a misapplication, a pet, a neighbor's garden — you may have no insurer to claim against.

**Competence, category by category.** A license is not general. Someone licensed for structural work is not thereby licensed to treat your lawn, your ornamental trees or the weeds on your driveway. The Board notes that applicators have been found reporting applications in categories they do not hold. Asking which categories a company holds is a completely normal question, and the answer tells you whether they can legally do the job you are asking about.

**Records.** Licensed applicators work to label and keep records of what was applied and where. If you sell the house, rent it out, or have a family member react to something, that record exists.

## Questions that separate operators

1. "What are you applying, and may I see the label?" A professional will show you without hesitation.
2. "Which category does this work fall under, and do you hold it?"
3. "What will you leave me in writing after the visit?"
4. "What did you find that is not a pest problem?" The answer tells you whether they inspected or just treated.
5. "What happens if it comes back inside the service period, and what voids it?"

## What the Board does not decide

Pricing, scheduling, guarantee terms and workmanship are between you and the contractor. Licensing is a floor, not a ranking — it tells you someone is permitted to do the work, not that they will do it well. Reviews, references and a written scope cover the rest.

## Reporting a problem

If you believe a pesticide was misapplied, the [Maine Board of Pesticides Control](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/php/pesticides/) takes complaints directly. That route exists whoever did the work, including us.

## The license categories in full

The Board issues commercial certification by category, and an applicator may hold several. The ones a Maine household or business is most likely to encounter:

- 1A and 1B — agricultural, animal and plant
- 2 — forest pest management
- 3A — outdoor ornamentals · 3B — turf · 3C — indoor ornamentals
- 5A — aquatic pest control
- 6A — right-of-way vegetation · 6B — general vegetation management
- 7A — structural general pest control, including food processing
- 7B — structural fumigation · 7C — disinfectant and biocide treatments
- 7D — wood preserving
- 7E — biting fly and other arthropod vectors, including ticks
- 7F — termite pests

A company treating your house for mice, treating the yard for ticks, and spraying the weeds in your driveway is working across three different categories. It is entirely reasonable to ask whether they hold all three.

## Recertification, and why it is a quality signal

Certification lasts three years. A Commercial Master must earn nine continuing-education credits within that period and an Operator six, through training run by the Board, the University of Maine Cooperative Extension, or trade organizations. It is not onerous, and it does mean an applicator who has kept current has sat through recent training on products, safety and regulation.
