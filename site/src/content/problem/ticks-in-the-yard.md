The useful question about ticks is not how many are on the property but where people and pets meet them.

## The yard changes that matter

1. Keep the lawn mown; short grass dries out and ticks do poorly in it.
2. Clear leaf litter in spring, especially along the wood line and in beds against the house.
3. Lay a three-foot barrier of wood chips or gravel where lawn meets woods.
4. Move play equipment, seating and the dog run out of the shaded edge.
5. Move bird feeders away from where people spend time — they concentrate the rodents that carry the nymphs.
6. Stack firewood off the ground, away from the house, in the sun if possible.
7. Prune to let light into the edge; humidity is what holds ticks there.

## What treatment adds

A targeted application to the edge habitat reduces what is present in that band for a period of weeks. Timed to the nymph window in late spring and the adult surge in autumn, it covers the two periods when unnoticed bites are most likely. It does not stop ticks arriving on deer, and a programme that claims otherwise is selling certainty it does not have.

## After time outdoors

Check at the hairline, behind the ears and knees, the waistband and the armpits, and check pets before they come inside. A tick removed early is the single most effective thing in this entire page.

## The edge is where the money is

If you do one thing on this page, make it the three-foot barrier where lawn meets woods, and keep that boundary cut back. It costs a weekend and a few bags of chips, and it does more for tick contact than a fourth application would.

## What a walk-through produces

We mark the edge zones on a sketch of the property, list the habitat items in priority order, note where children and pets actually spend time, and say which parts of the yard we would treat and which we would leave alone. Vegetable beds, flowering plants, and any area you tell us to avoid are recorded as exclusions before anything is applied.

## A property walk, in order

1. **Map the edge.** Walk the line where mown grass meets woods, brush, stone wall or field, and note where it comes closest to the house, the patio and the play area.
2. **Look at the ground cover.** Leaf litter, ivy, pachysandra and dense ornamental beds against the foundation hold humidity, which is what ticks need.
3. **Find the rodent traffic.** Stone walls, woodpiles, sheds, bird feeders and compost concentrate the mice and chipmunks that carry the nymphs.
4. **Look up.** Dense shade keeps the ground damp all day. Thinning it changes the microclimate more than any product does.
5. **Map where people actually go.** The dog run, the swing set, the shortcut to the mailbox, the path to the shed.

The plan that comes out of that walk is mostly free, and the treatment is layered onto it rather than sold in place of it.

## The barrier, in practical terms

Three feet of wood chips or gravel where lawn meets woods, kept clear of leaf litter, maintained once a season. It works because it is dry, hot and unshaded — conditions ticks cross poorly. It also marks the boundary for children and for dogs, which is half the value.

## If you find an attached tick

Use fine-tipped tweezers, grasp as close to the skin as possible, and pull steadily upward without twisting or jerking. Clean the site. Do not use heat, petroleum jelly or nail polish. Note the date. If a rash or fever follows in the weeks afterwards, that is a conversation for your doctor, and knowing roughly when the bite happened helps them.

## What treatment adds, in plain terms

A staged edge treatment reduces the number of ticks questing in the band you and your family actually cross. It is measurable in that band and it does not extend to the whole property, the woods beyond it, or the deer that walk through at night. Anyone who tells you otherwise is describing a result nobody can deliver in Maine.
