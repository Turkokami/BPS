Oxford covers nearly 39 square miles and holds about 4,200 people, which means most properties here have trees on at least one boundary and a good many have water on another. Thompson Lake runs the length of the town, the Little Androscoggin drains it, and the seasonal population swells around the lake, the speedway and fair week.

## Camps and closed-up buildings

A building that nobody enters between October and May is the single most reliable rodent habitat in this county. It is warm enough, it is undisturbed, and it usually contains exactly what a mouse wants: soft furnishings, stored bedding, paper, and no human traffic at all.

The work that prevents it happens before the building closes rather than after it opens:

1. Seal the exterior while the weather still allows a proper repair — sills, vents, utility penetrations, the gap under the door.
2. Remove or seal anything that can be nested in, and store fabrics in hard containers rather than bags.
3. Deal with the woodpile: off the ground, away from the wall, and never stacked against the building over winter.
4. Set monitoring so that what happens over the winter is visible in spring rather than guessed at.
5. Book the opening-up inspection for when you return, so the first weekend is not spent cleaning.

## Living against the tree line

Properties here mostly end at woods, brush or water, and that boundary is where the pest pressure sits. Ticks concentrate in the leaf litter and the first few feet of cover. Rodents travel along stone walls and wood edges. Wasps and hornets build in eaves, sheds and under decks that back onto shade.

That is a habitat conversation as much as a treatment one, and the report you get from us will say which of the two would do more for your property.
