Bath is a small city by population and a dense one by Maine standards: roughly 8,800 people on nine square miles of land, with houses close together and most of them owner-occupied. That density is the thing that shapes pest work here.

## An old city, house by house

The Census Bureau's American Community Survey for 2020–2024 puts 57.5% of Bath's housing units — 2,756 of 4,796 — in buildings dating from 1939 or earlier. That is a higher share than Portland, and it means the typical Bath call is about a house that has been standing for the best part of a century and has been opened up, extended and patched many times since.

Most of that stock is single houses rather than flats: the same survey counts 59.7% of units as detached houses, with a little over a third in buildings of two or more units. So unlike a city where the apartment block is the unit of work, in Bath it is usually one household and one old structure, with a neighbour's equally old structure a few yards away.

A house built before 1940, wherever it stands, tends to offer pests the same handful of openings, and these are the ones we check first:

- Stone or older masonry foundations with mortar gaps at the sill
- Sill plates that sit close to grade, where soil and mulch have crept up over the years
- Bulkhead doors and old service hatches that no longer close tightly
- Additions built onto the original house, leaving a joint between two foundations
- Attics with old insulation that shows every rodent run once someone looks

## Carpenter ants and the water they follow

UMaine Cooperative Extension explains that carpenter ants excavate rather than eat wood, and that infestations often begin with a water leak around a chimney, roof valley, gutter, window or door frame — with sill areas especially at risk where soil touches the wood. In a Bath house of the age described above, that list reads like an inspection checklist.

Extension's carpenter ant guidance also gives a useful rule for homeowners: in Maine, a wingless ant about three-quarters of an inch long is almost certainly a queen carpenter ant. Finding one indoors in late winter, along with coarse sawdust under trim or a baseboard, is the point to have the framing looked at rather than to buy another can of spray. ## Close neighbours, shared pressure

When properties sit on small lots with shared fence lines, mature trees and adjoining garages, a rodent or carpenter ant population is rarely confined to one address. A woodpile against a wall, an unsecured bin, a neglected shed two doors down — each of them sustains pressure that arrives at your foundation regardless of what you do inside your own house.

We will say so when we see it. It is an awkward conversation and it is more useful than another month of trapping.

## The new-owner inspection

The most common way a Bath household discovers a pest problem is by buying the house. A home inspection covers structure and systems; it is not a pest inspection, and a seller who lived with a mouse problem for three winters is not obliged to describe it.

We did exactly this job here about a year ago: a family had just bought a home, found it was dealing with both ants and mice, and treated it themselves with retail aerosols before calling. That is the normal sequence and it is the wrong one, because the products scatter the population and make the evidence harder to read.

If you have just moved in, the inspection to ask for is the one that covers:

- Sill line, bulkhead and crawlspace access on the exterior
- Utility penetrations — water service, electrical entry, dryer vent, hose bibs
- Attic insulation for travel paths and droppings
- Window and door framing for carpenter ant frass
- Basement joists and any wood in ground contact

## Sagadahoc is tick country

Bath sits in one of the counties Maine CDC flags for tick-borne disease. The agency's 2024 report, on preliminary figures, puts Sagadahoc County's Lyme disease rate at 369.9 cases per 100,000 residents against 230 for Maine as a whole, and lists it among the eight counties above the state rate for the second consecutive year. The count has climbed from 27 cases in 2020 to 139 in 2024.

The UMaine Cooperative Extension Tick Lab's 2025 testing points the same way: 45 of the 124 blacklegged ticks submitted from Sagadahoc County and tested (36.3%) carried the Lyme bacterium, and three carried Powassan virus. Three ticks is a small number, but at 2.4% it was the highest Powassan share of any county in that year's table.

For a Bath property, that makes [tick control](/services/tick-control/) a reasonable part of a spring visit rather than an afterthought — concentrated on the shaded edges, stone walls and leaf litter where ticks wait, and paired with the yard changes that make those edges less hospitable.

## What the seasons bring here

Coastal Sagadahoc County runs a compressed pest year. Carpenter ant activity indoors in late winter usually means a colony inside the structure rather than visitors. Ant swarms and the first ticks arrive with the spring thaw. Midsummer is wasps and mosquitoes. Then the first cold nights move rodents toward buildings, and a house that was quiet all summer starts producing noise overhead in October.

Sealing work done in September is prevention. The same work in January is a repair with the animals already inside.
